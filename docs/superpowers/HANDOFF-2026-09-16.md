## 2. What happened, and the reasoning not in the code

### #59 — the expiry warning was asking a question nobody was running to hear

`schwab_login_warning` warned at `age >= 6 days`. But **the alert is delivered by a scan**, and
scans run only on trading sessions. A token created on a Sunday expires on a Sunday: it is never
six days old on a day anything is up to say so.

Replayed against the token that was live that morning — created 2026-09-13 14:42 UTC, expiring
**Sunday** 09-20 — the old rule left **seven consecutive scans silent** and then said `EXPIRED`
on Monday. **The `warn` branch was never reached at all.** The operator's first notice would have
been the failure.

`next_scheduled_scan_utc` walks the schedule forward off the real XNYS calendar, reading the same
two time lists `configure_scheduler` registers (the `scheduler_summary` precedent: the previous
version of that banner read the *fallback* fields and announced a scan at a time nothing ran). The
calendar is load-bearing, not convenient — a `mon-fri` step answers **Saturday**, and **Good
Friday** is a market holiday but not a federal one. Both cases are pinned and both kill a naive
implementation.

**The age rule was UNIONED with the new one, never replaced**, and within hours that proved to be
more than caution. On the *refreshed* token (expiring Wednesday 09-23 00:40 UTC) the **age rule
fires first**, at Tuesday 09:35 ET — the next-scan rule is still silent there, because the token
does survive the 10:00 ETF scan half an hour later. Replacing rather than unioning would have cost
25 minutes of notice. **The old rule is the binding constraint whenever expiry falls midweek,
which is now the common case.**

An unknown deadline degrades to exactly the old behaviour rather than to silence, and the calendar
failure is caught *inside* the walk, because letting it propagate would reach
`alert_schwab_login`'s blanket `except` and swallow an `EXPIRED` alert along with the deadline it
was only refining.

**Method note:** the fix was verified by **mutation**, not only by green. Disabling the next-scan
rule kills 2 tests; disabling the calendar check kills 5 — with 33 and 30 other tests still
passing, so both mutants are *valid*. A mutant that fails everything with a `NameError` proves
nothing.

**A dead branch got in and was caught.** The first implementation carried `if not times: return
None`, copied from the shape of its sibling `last_scheduled_scan_utc` — where `max()` on an empty
list genuinely raises. In the *walk*, no candidate is ever found and it returns `None` anyway, so
no test could distinguish its presence from its absence. Removed. **Copying a neighbour's shape is
how unkillable branches get in.**

### #60 — the analyst criterion, registered before its data

Open item 2 had read *"analyst-removal criterion undecided — but now measurable"* since the first
clean session. **Measurable-and-undecided is the state in which a number arrives and the criterion
gets written to fit it.**

What the data actually said when asked (§3 has the full numbers): one cohort, 1w, **11 rows
reached the analyst**, and it points *against* the analyst — its rejects beat its picks by
3.19pp. t ≈ 1.5, and it hangs almost entirely on SNDK at −7.67pp. Precisely the kind of number
that becomes a decision if nobody wrote the rule down first.

The registration is `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-16-analyst-necessity-preregistration.md`.

> **Null: keep.** Fires when the mean weekly spread (analyst-stopped − analyst-BUY, 1w excess vs
> SPY, among `technical_verdict='passed'` rows) is **≥ +2.0pp** over **≥ 8 weekly observations**
> with a bootstrap 90% interval excluding zero. Action: the analyst stops vetoing.

**Why the null is keep:** the analyst is nearly free — all 46 calls on 09-15 landed on
`gemini-3.1-flash-lite`, free tier, no fallback. Its cost is ~3 minutes of scan latency and no
money. So the question is *"is it worse than nothing?"*, not *"does it earn its keep?"*. §0 states
explicitly that a non-firing result is **not** evidence the analyst helps.

**§2 is the load-bearing section, and the part most worth carrying elsewhere: the unit of
independence is the ISO WEEK, not the row.** At a 1-week horizon, consecutive sessions' windows
overlap by four trading days, and every name in a session shares one window. ~17 passers/session
*looks* like 85 points a week and is closer to **one**. Measured per-observation sd is **2.53pp**,
so 3pp is detectable in ~2 weekly observations, **2pp in ~4–8**, 1pp in ~25+, and anything under
~1pp is **permanently unreachable for this system**. The threshold sits where detection is
possible, because a criterion demanding 1pp would never fire — keep-forever disguised as a
deferral.

**An incidental finding, now pinned in §5: `analyst_confidence` carries zero information on the
reject side.** All **57** HOLD/SKIP verdicts through 09-15 are `medium`, no exceptions; all 15
`high` rows are BUYs. Any future rule weighting "high-confidence rejects" would weight a constant.

**Known weakness, recorded in §8 rather than discovered later:** the §5-style test asserting
runtime config still matches the frozen parameters is **not built**. Registering before the
cohort existed mattered more, and code lands slower than a document. Until it exists, §4.1 is
checked by hand.

## 6. Method notes from this session

- **Ask the rows what the data says before deciding what to build.** The analyst question looked
  like "run the report"; the report said 11 usable rows on a dead pipeline. That is what turned
  the task from *decide* into *preregister*.
- **State the null out loud, in the document.** A criterion whose default outcome is unstated gets
  read as evidence for whichever side the reader prefers. #60 §0 says in its own voice that a
  non-firing result is not evidence the analyst helps.
- **Set a threshold where detection is possible, then say so.** The power calculation is not
  decoration: a 1pp criterion here would be a decision to keep the analyst forever, written as
  though it were a deferral.
- **Check whether a test that passes on arrival is load-bearing.** Two did; one of them was
  pinning a branch that turned out to be dead, and removing the branch left the test still
  passing — which is the signal.
- **Union, don't replace, when widening a rule.** The Schwab age rule looked superseded and was
  kept only for safety. Hours later, on the refreshed token, it became the binding constraint.
- **A stale index line is the same failure as a stale handoff.** Three claims in `MEMORY.md` went
  wrong within one session — "no open PRs", the cache-rebuild expectation, and the Schwab item
  listed as open. Reconcile the index when the state it describes changes, not later.
- **Don't extend a mechanism to serve a check the check does not need.** Keeping the bot alive for
  §1b would have broken the §1b item that tests whether the bot exits.
